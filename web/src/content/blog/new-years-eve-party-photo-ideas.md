The best New Year's Eve party photos come from a plan made before the doors open: one or two moments you want to catch, a person assigned to midnight, and a way for everyone's photos to end up in one place. Below is a night in order, with a shot list for each stretch, then how to collect what your guests took.

## Decide what the night should look like in photos

Most hosts end up with the same picture: a blurry crowd at midnight, taken from the back. Avoid it by choosing, before the night, what you want to remember. Pick three:

- The room before anyone arrives, with the table set.
- A group of friends mid-laugh.
- The countdown from inside the room, not from behind phones.
- The very last people, still talking at 2 a.m.

Photos of faces and hands beat photos of decorations. If a photo would still make you smile next January, take it.

## Shot list, hour by hour

Parties run on a clock, and so should the photos. Here is a plan for a night that starts at 8 p.m.

**Before guests arrive (7:30)**
1. The table or the snacks, from above.
2. The room with every light on, then again with the lamps only.
3. A photo of whoever is helping you set up.

**Arrivals (8:00 to 9:00)**
4. Each guest at the door, in the first minute. Say "one for the roll" and keep it quick.
5. Coats pile, shoes by the door, the first drink being poured.

**Dinner or the first hours (9:00 to 11:00)**
6. Hands holding glasses over the table.
7. A toast that goes wrong.
8. Two people deep in conversation who do not know they are being photographed.
9. A group of four on the sofa, shot from across the room.

**The run-up to midnight (11:00 to 11:50)**
10. The clock, with people looking at it.
11. Someone handing out the noisemakers or the champagne.
12. A wide shot of the room, once, so you have it.

**Midnight**
13. The countdown, from a spot where you can see faces.
14. The first hug.
15. Everyone with a glass up.

**After midnight**
16. The cleanup crew, mid-song.
17. The last person to leave.

That is about 17 photos. One photo an hour as the host would be roughly a dozen, which is plenty; the list is a menu, not a quota.

## Settle midnight before midnight

Midnight is where the plan usually breaks, because the host is in the middle of it. Do this instead:

- **Name two photographers.** Ask them in the afternoon: "At 11:50, would you take the countdown?" Two, in case one is mid-hug.
- **Tell them where to stand.** A corner of the room that sees most faces, not the back of the crowd.
- **Say how many shots.** Three or four. The countdown, the kiss, the toast. Then they put the phone away.
- **Keep the lights on.** A phone camera in a dark room guesses. Leave a lamp lit and turn the flash on.

If you are inside the circle, enjoy it. Nobody wishes they had a better photo of themselves checking their phone at midnight.

## Get close, use the flash, keep a wall behind

Parties are dim, and phones struggle with dim. Three habits fix most of it:

- **Flash on.** The harsh look is part of the charm, and it keeps faces from turning to mud.
- **Within a few steps of people.** Zooming in at a party makes soft, dark photos. Walk closer instead.
- **A wall or a doorway behind them.** The flash lights something solid and the background does not disappear.

Fireworks are the exception: you will not catch them well on a phone, and you miss the fireworks while trying. Take one photo of the faces looking up instead.

## Hand the camera around

One photographer produces one view of the night. A room of twenty people produces twenty. The easiest way to get candid photos is to stop being the only person responsible for them.

Ask each guest for a small number of shots, a handful or a dozen, so the photos feel like a few good moments, not a feed. Give a small prompt if they want one: "a photo of someone you only see once a year", "the best outfit", "the first person to cry at midnight". Prompts like these are the paper version of what a [photo mission](/missions/) does.
