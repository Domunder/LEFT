A wedding photo scavenger hunt is a short list of moments you ask guests to photograph during the day. Below are 40 prompts you can copy, sorted by when they happen, then the rules that keep the game kind and three ways to hand it out: cards on the tables, one sign, or an app.

Do not give anyone all 40. Pick ten to twelve per guest, and give different tables different lists. More on that after the prompts.

## Getting ready and arrival

1. Your outfit in the mirror before you leave the house
2. The invitation, wherever it has lived for the past few months
3. The view on the way there
4. The first hug in the parking lot
5. A detail someone clearly spent hours on
6. The welcome sign, with a guest who does not know they are in the photo

## Ceremony

If the ceremony is unplugged, keep these for the minutes before it starts and the minutes after it ends. All six work that way.

7. The seats before anyone sits down
8. Someone fixing someone else's tie, collar or hair
9. A flower girl or ring bearer off duty
10. Something borrowed or something blue
11. Petals or confetti in the air
12. The first hug after the ceremony

## Cocktail hour

13. A drink in the best light you can find
14. Two people meeting for the first time
15. Three generations in one photo
16. Someone wearing the same color as you
17. The guest who traveled the farthest
18. A new ring next to an old ring
19. Someone you only knew from stories until today

## Dinner

20. Your place card and whoever is sitting beside it
21. The plate before the first bite
22. Faces in the room during a speech, not the speaker
23. Glasses mid-clink
24. A parent of the couple laughing
25. The cake before the knife
26. Speech notes, folded and well handled

## Dance floor

27. A dance circle from the inside
28. Someone singing every word
29. The couple, blurred on purpose
30. A grandparent on the dance floor
31. The band or DJ from the side
32. Hands in the air
33. A jacket left on the back of a chair

## End of the night

34. The late-night snack
35. A tie that is no longer around a neck

37. A goodbye hug

40. The ride home

## Rules that keep it kind

A scavenger hunt goes wrong in two ways: guests chase photos instead of being at the wedding, or a prompt turns someone into the joke. Five rules prevent both. Put them on the card in a line or two.

- **People before points.** If someone says no to a photo, that is the end of it. No prompt is worth asking twice.
- **Nothing you would not show them.** Leave out prompts that depend on someone being drunk, upset or caught off guard in a way they would mind. "Someone singing every word" is funny for everyone. "The most wasted guest" is not.
- **Phones down when it counts.** No hunting during the vows, the speeches or the first dance unless the prompt is about that moment, and then one photo is enough.
- **Stay out of the photographer's way.** Guests shoot from where they are. Nobody steps into the aisle for a prompt.
- **Ask before photographing children.** A quick word with a parent first.

It also helps to say that the game is optional. Some guests will finish the list by dessert, some will take two photos all night, and both are fine.

## How to run a wedding photo scavenger hunt

**Announce it once, at the right time.** The start of cocktail hour works well: people are standing around with a drink and nothing to do. One sentence from the officiant, the DJ or a friend is enough. "There is a card on your table with ten photos we would love you to take tonight."

**Keep each list short.** A five-hour reception is long, but nobody wants homework. Ten to twelve prompts per guest leaves room to take photos that are not on any list. With 80 guests at ten tables, four different cards of ten prompts cover all 40, and neighboring tables are not all pointing their phones at the same grandparent.

**Decide where the photos go before the day.** This is the part most lists skip. A hunt without a destination leaves the photos on 80 phones. Print it on the card: a shared album, a link or a QR code. [A QR code for wedding photos](/qr-code-for-wedding-photos/) on the same card as the prompts is the least effort for guests.

Then pick a format.

### Wedding photo challenge cards

One card per place setting or a small stack per table. Print the prompts as a checklist, with the rules and the album link on the back. Cards work best when you want different lists at different tables, and guests can slip them into a pocket and take them to the dance floor.

### A sign, or an "I spy" wedding game

One framed sign near the bar or the guest book that starts "I spy with my little eye" and lists ten to fifteen things to find. It costs one print and suits a small wedding where everyone passes the same spot. The downside is that everyone has the same list, and nobody remembers it once they walk away. Keep it to prompts that are easy to recall.

### Wedding photo bingo

The same prompts in a grid, three by three or four by four. A full row is a win. Bingo adds a finish line, which some groups love and some find a little much for a wedding. If you use it, make the prize small, and shuffle the squares between cards so the tables are not racing for the same row.

### An app

An app replaces the printing and solves the "where do the photos go" problem at the same time, because the photo is taken where it will be collected. It suits weddings where most guests are comfortable scanning a code. For a crowd that would rather hold a card, paper is the better choice, and you can do both.

## Where LEFT fits

LEFT is a disposable camera on your guests' phones. They join by link or QR code with no sign-up and get a limited roll of photos, for example 24 each. Nobody sees any photo until the wedding is over. Then the whole group's film is developed at once.

Its [missions](/missions/) are a scavenger hunt handed out privately. The host switches them on in the app and edits the list, so you can type in prompts from this page. Every guest gets one to three when the event starts, and nobody knows who got which. After the reveal, each mission's photo shows up in the album.

That is a different game from a card of ten. One to three prompts per guest is a nudge, not a checklist, and there is no grid to fill and no winner. If you want bingo, print bingo. If you want each guest quietly looking for one or two moments, missions do that without anything on the tables. They are free on every event. LEFT itself is free for up to 50 guests on a limited roll, and from $14.99, paid once by the host, for bigger weddings.

Whichever format you pick, it comes down to a short list, the five rules and one place for the photos. In LEFT, that is missions switched on and a film that is developed when the wedding is over.
