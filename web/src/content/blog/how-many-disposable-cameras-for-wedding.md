How many disposable cameras for a wedding? Plan **one camera for every 8 guests**, rounded up. That is roughly one per table, with a few left over for the bar and the dance floor. For 100 guests it means 13 cameras and 351 exposures. Go down to one per 10 guests if a photographer covers the whole evening, and up to one per 5 if the cameras are the main record of the reception.

## How many disposable cameras for a wedding, by guest count

A typical single-use camera has 27 exposures. The "exposures" column is the standard plan multiplied by 27.

| Guests | Light (1 per 10) | Standard (1 per 8) | Generous (1 per 5) | Exposures, standard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 108 |
| 50 | 5 | 7 | 10 | 189 |
| 80 | 8 | 10 | 16 | 270 |
| 100 | 10 | 13 | 20 | 351 |
| 150 | 15 | 19 | 30 | 513 |
| 200 | 20 | 25 | 40 | 675 |

Pick the column by what else is photographing the day:

- **Light** if a photographer stays until the end and the cameras are a bonus.
- **Standard** for most weddings: a photographer for part of the day, cameras for the rest.
- **Generous** if there is no photographer at the reception, or the reception runs longer than five hours.

## Why one camera per 8 guests

Work it out from the frames, not from the cameras. One camera shared by 8 people is about 3 photos per guest. That sounds small, and it is the right size: at any table two or three people take most of the pictures and the rest take none. Three frames per head, spent unevenly, is a camera that comes back full instead of half used.

It also matches the room. Most reception tables seat 8 to 10, so "one per 8" and "disposable cameras per table" land on nearly the same number. The difference is the spares. A camera on a table photographs that table. The ones people remember are taken at the bar, outside, and on the dance floor, so keep two or three cameras that belong to no table and put them there after dinner.

## Why the estimates you find differ so much

Search this question and you will see anything from 4 to 25 cameras for 100 guests. The answers are not wrong so much as answers to different questions.

- **They count different things.** One per table, one per couple and one per guest are all common rules. For 100 guests they give about 10, 50 and 100 cameras.
- **Some assume a photographer and some do not.** A few cameras as a side activity and cameras as the only coverage are different jobs.
- **Some count frames taken, others photos worth keeping.** Not every frame is a keeper: thumbs over the lens, the flash left off, a camera left half shot. If half of your 351 frames turn out, you have about 175 photos, which is still a full album.

Low estimates usually picture a table activity next to a professional. High ones picture every guest with a camera of their own. One per 8 sits between them on purpose.

## How many disposable cameras for 150 guests, worked through

Say 150 guests at 16 tables, a photographer booked until the first dance, and a reception that runs five hours.

1. 150 divided by 8 is 18.75, so 19 cameras.
2. 16 go on the tables before dinner.
3. 3 stay back: one at the bar, two handed to people on the dance floor once the photographer has left.
4. 19 times 27 is 513 exposures, a little over 3 per guest.

If the photographer stays to the end, 15 is plenty. With no photographer after the ceremony, go to 30 and give the extra cameras to people you trust to use them.

## Disposable camera wedding cost: the formula

The price on the box is only half of it. Film has to be developed, and most couples want scans as well as negatives.

**Total = cameras × (price per camera + developing and scans per camera)**

Before you buy, get three numbers:

- The price per camera for the model with a built-in flash. Indoors, a camera without one is close to useless.
- The lab's price to develop one camera and scan it, and how long it takes.
- Shipping to the lab and back, if it is not local.

Then multiply by the number in the table. Developing is charged per camera whether it was fully shot or not, which is the practical reason not to over-order: 10 cameras that come back full cost less than 20 that come back half empty, and give you the same photos.

## Getting the cameras used, and getting them back

- Put them out before dinner, not before the ceremony.
- A small sign with three instructions: turn the flash on, stand close, leave the camera on the table.
- Have someone at the microphone mention them once.
- Give guests something to look for. A short list of prompts on the table helps; our [wedding photo scavenger hunt](/blog/wedding-photo-scavenger-hunt/) has one ready to print.
- Number the cameras on the bottom and name one person to collect them at the end of the night. Count them.

In short: count the tables, add three, and ask the lab for its price before you order.
